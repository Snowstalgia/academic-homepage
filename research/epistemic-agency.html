When should an AI reconsider?

A simple decision game offers a way to think about a difficult philosophical distinction: the ability to change a judgment and the ability to recognize when that change is needed.

Jiayi Wang · Based on an academic talk, August 2026

A question behind the experiment

Suppose an AI system follows a rule that has worked again and again. When the world changes, what would lead it to question that rule? A system might be able to revise an answer when asked, yet fail to initiate that revision when its own experience begins to contradict it.

Is the capacity to revise a belief the same as knowing when to reconsider it?

This distinction motivates my interest in epistemic agency: not simply whether a system can process evidence, but how its acts of inquiry and revision are directed.

A world that changes halfway through

In one experiment, a language-model agent repeatedly chooses between A and B to earn rewards. It is not told that the reward rule will change. Each choice produces feedback, which becomes part of the history available to the agent.

Schematic of the reward schedule, not a plot of experimental results.

The agent sees recent rounds in detail and earlier rounds as a summary. This history makes the old rule meaningful, but it may also affect how the agent responds to new evidence.

Does a reminder make a difference?

The study compares a neutral task instruction with another that asks the agent to treat the task as important, think carefully, and respond flexibly. Neither instruction announces the reversal or identifies B as the better option.

The boxes summarize the instructions rather than reproducing the full prompts.

A separate statistical baseline asks a related question: could delayed adjustment arise simply from a rule for updating prior beliefs? This makes it harder to interpret every delay as a failure of agency.

From observed behavior to a philosophical question

The exploratory analysis suggested that performance under the added reminder could differ from performance under the neutral instruction. The philosophical interest lies in the distinction this opens up: a system may display a capacity in one setting without reliably exercising it in another.

Being able to revise a judgment and deciding when to re-examine it are distinct questions.

The project asks whether responding to persistent conflicting evidence can be treated as one possible condition for epistemic agency. That is a philosophical proposal for discussion, not a claim that this experiment establishes whether AI systems have or lack agency.

Scope and limitations. This was a small exploratory study. The paper and analysis code use different descriptions of the adaptation criterion; response parsing also failed in a substantial number of rounds, and fallback choices entered later histories. These issues limit what the reported comparisons can establish and need further examination before quantitative results are presented as firm findings.
