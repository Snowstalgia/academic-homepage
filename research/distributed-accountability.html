When the rules stop asking questions

What if a multi-agent system keeps operating within its formal rules, while its collective performance moves away from the goal it was designed to serve?

Jiayi Wang · Based on an academic talk, May 2026

Beyond asking who caused the outcome

In a distributed system, different agents can act under different objectives. Understanding a collective outcome requires us to ask how roles, incentives, information and institutional rules fit together. My research asks a further question: who is responsible for reviewing the rules that determine when an explanation is required?

If no accountability rule is triggered, can an organization conclude that its collective goal is being met?

A public-goods game

In an exploratory simulation, a coordinator recommends a contribution and two worker agents each decide how much to contribute. Contributions are multiplied and shared. Each worker has an incentive to keep resources for itself, while higher joint contributions increase the team's total payoff.

The simulation has three consecutive ten-round stages. Strategy guidance is updated between stages. A separate explanation procedure is triggered only when total contributions fall below a fixed threshold.

What can a threshold tell us?

In the recorded run, average team payoff declined across the three stages, while the explanation procedure was activated only once, in the first stage. This gap follows in part from the scope of the rule: a threshold designed to flag very low contributions does not cover every outcome below the collective maximum.

A conceptual diagram, not a general measure of organizational performance.

The distinction is not proof that the agents learned to evade oversight. It is a reason to examine what the rule was meant to detect and whether other review processes exist.

From an action to the rules around it

A silent procedure can answer a narrow question without settling the broader question of whether an organization is meeting its goals.

My philosophical argument is conditional: when a shared goal is justified, people have reasonable access to relevant information and authority to act, and other channels are inadequate, the choice of trigger itself may need review. The question is about the responsibility to assess a rule, not a claim that every suboptimal outcome is a moral failure.

Scope and limitations. The material comes from one dependent sequence without independent replications or a fixed-prompt control. Two parsing failures introduced default values into the run and subsequent history. The case cannot establish that prompt updates caused the observed decline, that agents intentionally avoided accountability, or that a stable equilibrium emerged.
